What he takes on

Broken equipment, cracked frames, and things that need making.

Most of it is repair work on machines that have to go back to work the same day. The rest is fabrication — parts and pieces built to fit, because nobody sells the thing you actually need.

Heavy equipment

Excavator buckets rebuilt with new wear bars and teeth, cracked booms welded up, pins and bushings sorted out. Machines that crack because they get used hard.

Trucks & trailers

Axles, hitch plates, pintle hooks and receivers, frame rails, and whole trailer decks laid back down in steel. Including the kind of thing that stops a rig at the roadside.

Aluminium, in the field

Not every mobile welder will touch aluminium away from a shop. This one does it on site, which is usually the whole reason somebody is calling.

Custom fabrication

Made and fitted to the job: handrails, brackets, plates, frames, guards. If you can describe it or sketch it, it is worth a phone call.

Structural & pipe

Beams, box tubing and pipe — including steel welded back into the chassis under something that has started to sag.

Fleets and yards

Standing work for outfits that run equipment hard and need the same person back each time, rather than whoever picks up the phone.

How it works

He travels to the machine. That is the whole idea.

The truck carries the welder, the reels, the bottles and the tools. What would normally mean loading a broken machine onto a trailer and hauling it somewhere instead means somebody turning up where it already is.

Call and say what broke

What it is, what it is made of, and where it is sitting. That is usually enough to say yes or no on the phone.

He comes out to it

Jobsite, yard, shoulder of the road, back of a farm. One customer had him come two hours out to a trailer that had let go.

It goes back to work

Most of this work exists so a machine or a rig can finish the day it was already in the middle of.
